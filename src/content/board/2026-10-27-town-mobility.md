problem: "Self-driving taxi fleets are arriving in cities over the next few years. In a town of about 100,000 people, with an urban core and outlying villages, poor buses, and most households owning one or two cars, how might we make getting people to what they need far better for everyone, rather than ending up with one company owning the dispatch and charging a toll on every trip?"
---

**Make the town’s transport network easy to join and hard to capture: shared access, competing operators, and a guaranteed way home.** Buy access to work, care, shops and friends; let buses, taxis, community vehicles and eventually autonomous fleets compete to provide it.

Assumption: this is a proposed local design; driverless service becomes useful gradually and may reach villages later than the urban core.

The purpose is to give everyone reliable access without making car ownership compulsory. Today, households supply most vehicles and carry their costs; poor buses leave people who cannot drive dependent on lifts, while villages have too little concentrated demand for frequent conventional services.

The scarce resource is not just vehicles. It is reliable coordination: knowing that a journey out includes a way back, that assistance will be available, and that changing provider will not mean losing access.

### 1. Changed arrangements

**A. A town mobility exchange with several doors — imagined locally.**

Create a publicly accountable exchange that operators can join on published, non-exclusive terms. People book through competing apps, a telephone service, or staffed local outlets. Operators can also take direct bookings. The exchange’s software, interfaces and operating contract must be transferable, so its contractor cannot become the new gatekeeper.

Give passengers, village representatives, disabled residents and operators a say in its rules. Publish fares, public payments and service performance; keep identifiable journey histories private. Fund shared coordination transparently rather than giving one supplier an uncapped percentage of every fare.

- **Physical account:** Electricity or fuel moves existing vehicles; vehicles and batteries circulate through use, maintenance and eventual material recovery; tyres, components and energy are consumed or lost; waste heat enters the surroundings and ultimately leaves Earth as radiation.
- **Opens:** Customers can change providers; small operators can reach demand without surrendering their businesses. Human-driven services can participate before autonomy arrives.
- **Protects and compounds:** Non-exclusive contracts preserve alternatives. Common booking and accessibility requirements make each new operator easier to add. Trial it on a few routes before expanding.
 **B. Reliable shared routes with a guaranteed last leg — imagined locally.**

Concentrate frequent shared services on the busiest corridors. Connect villages through timed minibuses, pooled taxis and bookable accessible vehicles. Offer recurring reservations for shifts, school or care appointments, plus a guaranteed return when a connection fails.

Pay separately for availability and completed journeys. Otherwise operators have a reason to serve easy urban trips and ignore expensive rural ones. Specify assistance as well as wheelchair space: an empty driver’s seat does not help someone who needs an escort.

- **Physical account:** Electricity or fuel carries passengers; vehicles, repairable parts and batteries return through maintenance loops; tyre material and replacement parts leak from those loops; propulsion and charging losses become heat. Pooling can reduce vehicle distance, but detours and empty repositioning must be counted.
- **Opens:** Dependable access for village residents, non-drivers and shift workers. Once reliability is demonstrated, some households can choose to give up a second car.
- **Protects and compounds:** Keep existing services during the trial. Repeated bookings reveal where a fixed route would work better than individual taxis.
 **C. Bring some destinations to people — imagined locally; this changes the question.**

Use village halls and existing shops for coordinated deliveries, collection points, visiting services and suitable remote appointments. Let employers offer shared transport or staggered shifts. Keep travel available where the journey itself matters or a remote substitute is inadequate.

- **Physical account:** Electricity powers buildings and communications; shared deliveries consolidate goods movement; equipment and reusable containers circulate through repair and reuse; packaging and worn components leave the loop; buildings, computers and vehicles release heat.
- **Opens:** Access without a journey, especially for carers and people with limited mobility. It also frees transport capacity for trips that cannot be replaced.
- **Protects and compounds:** Participation stays voluntary. Shared premises make further services cheaper to introduce; preserve in-person provision until alternatives prove useful.
 **Choose A as the organising move, with B as its first service and C reducing avoidable demand.** Open booking alone does not guarantee affordable rural access; a rural subsidy alone does not prevent platform capture. Together they address both.

### 2. One step now

An employer or clinic can start this week by using an existing licensed taxi operator to guarantee return journeys for a small, consenting group already struggling with transport. Cluster compatible bookings and retain individual rides where pooling fails.

The first passenger gets a dependable journey on the first operating day; the operator gets a paid booking. Record costs, waits, missed journeys and assistance needs with consent. Use that evidence to recruit a second operator under the same terms. No new app is needed.

### 3. One long route

Build towards a mixed fleet over several years, adding autonomous vehicles only where they deliver the required service. The unresolved capability is dependable operation across local roads, weather and accessibility needs, with human assistance and recovery when needed.

Councils, major employers, healthcare providers and a resident-owned organisation could jointly back availability contracts and shared infrastructure. Lease vehicles initially to preserve flexibility. Keep dispatch, charging and depots available to multiple operators. Autonomy should lower the cost of an existing public purpose, not determine that purpose.

### 4. One opening for the next person

Design one village-to-town pilot using **actual, consented journey requests**: departure windows, return windows, assistance needs and current costs. Compare a scheduled minibus, pooled taxis and their combination, including empty mileage and guaranteed returns. Find the smallest funded service that makes a household comfortable relying on it.

### 5. A changed premise

**Replace “the only guardrail is irreversible loss” with “protect irreversible value, require consent, and prevent repeated reversible harms from accumulating”.**

An unaffordable fare, a missed shift or a stranded passenger may be individually reversible yet still make someone worse off. This change opens experimentation whose costs are carried by its backers rather than imposed on people with the fewest alternatives.
