## 1. It started with a drawing

The coil in `NOTES-circular-to-helical` was being turned into a mark: something a person could draw on a napkin. Tom was torn between two pictures of "up".

Tom:

> "I'm torn between the 'drift velocity' type 'up' and the helix itself going up"
>
> "what we're really saying is progress, growth, value is a drift velocity type approach"
>
> "there's a lot of movement, activity, general shenanigans in each cycle, but the net trend is up - towards increasing value for all OLU and less to none"
>
> "but it might even be a bit up and down along the way - we clearly can't really show the granularity of that"

("OLU" is "observers like us".) He suggested a zoomed-in section "showing the nanoscopic trials and tribulations, the ups and downs, the muckery and so on".

[ASSEMBLER] The assembler drew both and argued for keeping the coil with the drift inside it as a close-up. Its reasons:

- Drift alone is a scribble with an arrow through it. Nobody can redraw it, and it loses "the same atoms come round again".
- The coil alone says every lap is a clean gain, and it isn't.
- In a wire, random motion gives no drift at all. Electrons drift only because a field pushes them one way. So "lots of shenanigans, net up" needs two things: energy, and something that keeps the good tries and drops the bad ones. Without the second, the sun just makes more muddle.

It called the close-up "the honest version". That turned out to be a mistake of scale, as §2 shows.

## 2. A try is not a delta

Tom, first on a larger thought he then set aside:

> "we COULD argue that the universe only supports things that are net beneficial anyway - as in, humans are partly an experiment, and if we end up not making stuff work better - i.e. capitalism - we will self-terminate"
>
> "that's more philosophical than we might need in a diagram though"

Then the rule:

> "the intention of configuration economics is for every delta to be better for at least one entity and worse for none"
>
> "maybe we don't really want the 'honest, learning by failure' model - not because it's not true, but because we're measuring at the scale that supports the notion of 'more possible configurations' and so on"
>
> "I guess we don't need the zooming in at all, really"

[ASSEMBLER] The assembler withdrew "the honest version". It had mixed up two things:

- **A try** is something attempted in the search: in a design, a model, a small test.
- **A delta** is a change actually made to the world.

Tom's rule is about deltas. Failed tries are not deltas; they are what was rejected before anything changed. The coil is drawn at the scale where the rule applies. The muddle is real, and it belongs to the search, which is where pattern intelligence earns its keep by making failures cheap.

This also says where the third term of Tom's equation sits in the drawing (same atoms × constant solar flux × pattern intelligence). The same atoms and the sun alone give a ring. What turns the ring into a coil is choosing the better arrangement each time round. Round is the atoms, the rays are the sun, and the rise is the selection. `NOTES-circular-to-helical` §4 says the same thing as "the rise per turn is where selection lives".

## 3. The objection an economist raises first

[ASSEMBLER] The assembler then raised a caution. "Worse for none" is the claim an economist will attack first. Almost every real change costs someone, usually whoever profits from the current arrangement.

Tom:

> "yes, but that's usually because the search space has not been expanded enough"
>
> "the current beneficiaries lose 'over what metrics, over what scale, over what timeframe, in which identity...'"

[ASSEMBLER] The assembler half agreed, and said the reply holds two different answers.

**The first is strong.** Economics already concedes half of it: where a change gains more in total than it costs, an arrangement in which nobody loses exists in principle, and the usual reply is that it is too expensive to find and negotiate. The claim here is that the finding has become cheap. That can be tested case by case.

**The second is dangerous if used as a verdict.** "You only lose on some metric, scale, timeframe or identity" is what everyone who ever imposed a cost has said: you will be better off in the long run, or as a member of society, or on the measure that really matters. It lets the proposer choose the loser's yardstick, and it makes "worse for none" impossible to disprove.

So the four questions keep their place and change their job. They are directions in which to expand the search. They are not grounds for telling someone they have not lost.

- Used as search: the oil firm loses as an oil extractor, so look for the arrangement where the people and the capital inside it are no worse off. Identity said where to look.
- Used as verdict: the oil firm "doesn't really lose, viewed over fifty years". That is overruling them.

The test the assembler proposed: the entity itself says it is no worse off, by its own account. Would they sign?

This work's own runs have already met the hard cases, and they do not all dissolve:

- `NOTES-iterative-search-mobility` ended on this question and left it open: is incumbent power defending rent a real floor, or an unexpanded search space?
- `NOTES-autonomous-fleet-six-hats` called the drivers "the wound that yields LEAST": a levy funds income and cannot reconstitute identity or dignity. There, identity is where the loss is.
- `NOTES-dna-economy-ogwen` says "never expect unanimity" and "route around the few".
- `NOTES-ratchet-cell-governance-ledger` found the real loser was the household that cannot take part, not any level of government.

## 4. Symmetry and continuity

Tom:

> "the bridge test has to work for everyone, because a bridge works for everyone - maybe not the fish!!!"
>
> "there are 2 very different things going on here - at least"
>
> "symmetry and continuity"
>
> "someone can appear to be winning right now, using the lens of symmetry - more than others, etc."
>
> "but in the lens of continuity, 'there's no real 'winning' if you're getting a better seat on a bus that's driving off a cliff'"
>
> "so, we have to have the evidence to show that this is better for everyone not just now, but at all conceivable times in the future"
>
> "because this move INCREASES possibility space, for all, including you"
>
> "so, it's not about making a person, wealthy in current metrics, move to the back of the bus"
>
> "it's about making sure the journey continues for longer, building a parachute, or a rocket engine, or an inflatable impact zone etc."

[ASSEMBLER] The assembler took this to be the real answer to the incumbent, and better than either of its own. It also removes the "verdict" problem from §3.

If a move takes away nothing anyone could do before and adds something, every person still has every choice they had. They are then no worse off by any measure they care to pick, over any timeframe, in any identity. Nobody has to choose their yardstick for them.

That is also how "at all conceivable times" can be met without forecasting. Evidence about every possible future cannot be collected, and the assembler pushed back on that phrase. The checkable form is narrower: this move closes nothing and opens something. This is not new to the work. It is `viable-objective` (contested), which already prefers moves that keep the reachable set open "for all major classes of life across all relevant horizons", applied to persons, with `asymmetry-of-option-space-change` (derived) as the reason closing is weighted so heavily.

Tom's two lenses become two tests, and a move has to pass both:

- **Symmetry, today.** Nobody moves to the back of the bus. This is the bridge test: each person no worse on day-one terms.
- **Continuity.** The journey goes on for longer, for all.

The second needs the first. Alone, the bus argument has a hole: a mortal passenger can say "the cliff is after my stop". Economists call that discounting, and it is rational. If the move also costs that passenger nothing today, the reply has no force.

Two things survive even this:

- **Rank.** If everyone's reach grows, nobody's reach shrinks, and the gap between them can still narrow. Someone who values being ahead, as such, does lose. The plain statement would be: this protects your reach, not your lead.
- **The energy budget.** Under bounded flux, an option that is still listed but can no longer be powered has not been kept. "Closes nothing" has to mean reachable.

## 5. Two quantities

Tom:

> "the notion of possible spaces - does every move increase both the number of possible spaces, and the capacity to generate further increases"
>
> "this is important"

[ASSEMBLER] The assembler's answer was no: not every move does both, and the difference matters. There are two quantities.

- **How many possibilities there are now.**
- **The capacity to generate more.**

A move can raise the first while spending down the second. Burning a stock does that: more options today, less ability to make options later. This gives Tom's cliff a precise meaning. The cliff is where the capacity to generate has run out while the possibilities still look plentiful, which is why it cannot be seen from the good seats.

The drawing already carries both. The rise is more possibilities. The widening of the turns is the growing capacity to generate them; they were widened so the coil would not read as a staircase, and this is the reason underneath that. `configuration-generates-configuration` (contested) is the claim that the second quantity exists and compounds. The propositions, the canon and the helical note were searched, and none of them states the two as separate things a move can trade against each other.

The assembler proposed a ranking of moves. It is the assembler's, and Tom has not ruled on it:

1. Never lower the capacity to generate more.
2. Good: raises what is possible now.
3. Best: raises the capacity itself.

## 7. Where it stands

[ASSEMBLER]

**Tom's, in his words:**

- Every delta is intended to be better for at least one entity and worse for none.
- Where someone loses, that is usually because the search space has not been expanded enough.
- Symmetry and continuity are different lenses, and winning in the first means little without the second.
- The aim is not to move anyone to the back of the bus. It is to make the journey continue for longer.

**The assembler's, offered and not ruled on:**

- A try is not a delta (§2).
- The four questions (metric, scale, timeframe, identity) are for searching, not for overruling (§3).
- "Closes nothing and opens something" as the checkable form of "better at all conceivable times" (§4).
- Two tests, symmetry today and continuity, both required (§4).
- Possibilities now and the capacity to generate more are two quantities, and the three-step ranking (§5).

**Not settled:** whether "worse for none" is a selection rule and a search target, or a claim that such a move always exists. The assembler's view is the first: keep searching until nobody has to lose, and say so where the search fails. The four cases in §3 are the record of this work doing that.

## 8. The strongest objections

[ASSEMBLER]

**1. "Closes nothing" may be almost never true.** Any real change uses land, materials, attention or energy that could have gone elsewhere. Read strictly, almost every move closes something for someone. If the rule passes almost nothing it is useless, and if "closes" is loosened it becomes the judgment call it was meant to replace.

**2. The fish.** Tom's own aside. A bridge works for everyone who crosses it, and "everyone" quietly meant people. `viable-objective` says "all major classes of life". A test that only asks whether each person would sign has no signature from the river.

**3. "Would they sign?" can be gamed.** A holder of a toll can refuse to sign anything, and then the test hands a veto to exactly the party it was meant to answer. `NOTES-four-candidates-and-the-capture-rule` already holds that a design whose median adopter needs forcing fails, and that the remainder is priced, not moralised. That is a different and weaker promise than "worse for none".

**4. A position whose value is other people's lack.** A monopoly toll is worth something only because others have fewer options. No expansion of the search finds a version its holder would sign. The framework can say that this is not value in its own terms, because it shrinks option space. That may be principled. It is also a definition doing the work, and an economist will say so.

**5. Rank is not a detail.** §4 concedes that this protects reach and not lead. Much of what people defend is their lead. If most real resistance is about rank, the two tests can both pass and the move can still be fought.

**6. The two quantities cannot be measured.** `option-space-measurability` is `open`: nobody can yet measure option space. A capacity to generate more option space is one step harder. Until that moves, the ranking in §5 orders things nobody can read off an instrument.

**7. The self-termination argument.** That the universe only supports what is net beneficial cannot be checked from inside it, and survivors would say it either way. Tom set it aside for the drawing. It should stay out of any argument meant to be one nobody can disagree with.

**8. This is one conversation.** Two voices, one of them a language model that was asked to take positions. No adversary ran against it. Agreement reached this way is weak evidence.

## 9. What this note does and does not show

[ASSEMBLER]

- **It shows** how a rule Tom stated in one line ("better for at least one entity and worse for none") stands up to the first objection it will meet, and what it had to become to stand: a rule over deltas, tested today and over time, with two named things it does not protect.
- **It does not show** that any real move passes both tests. That needs a worked case, and the four cases in §3 are closer to counter-examples than to confirmations.
- **Not checked:** the economics literature on Pareto improvement, compensation tests and positional goods, which has worked this ground for a century. The note uses those ideas in plain words and claims nothing new against them.
- **Opened for the register:** this is a reply to objection 4 of `NOTES-circular-to-helical` ("higher for whom?") and belongs with "who it rises for, and who sees from the edge" in `BACKLOG.md`.
